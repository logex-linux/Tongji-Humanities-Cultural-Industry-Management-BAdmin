**Satire and Social Critique: Analyzing Maggie Trinkle's "I (Don't) Want A Wife"**

Maggie Trinkle's "I (Don't) Want A Wife" (2020) is a satirical homage to Judy Brady's 1971 feminist classic. Through intertextual dialogue with Brady's original, Trinkle employs satire to criticize society's systemic failure during the pandemic while demonstrating that gender inequality persists after fifty years. Her rhetorical exigence—the pandemic's disproportionate burden on women—transforms Brady's domestic critique into a demand for institutional change.

Trinkle employs several satirical elements effectively. First, she uses irony of purpose: she repeatedly states she wants "a goddamn functioning society," but actually exposes how society has catastrophically failed women. The "I want a society..." repetition mirrors Brady's "I want a wife..." structure, redirecting demand from individual husbands to collective social responsibility. Third, Trinkle weaponizes pandemic-specific imagery—homeschooling, disinfecting groceries, sewing masks—exposing the "second shift" multiplied into a "fourth shift."

The essays share crucial similarities. Both address gender inequality and women's invisible labor. Both deploy anaphoric "I want..." repetition as their central rhetorical device. Both conclude with "My God..." exclamations signaling exasperation. However, differences reveal historical evolution. Brady targets individual husbands' expectations within marriage during early second-wave feminism. Trinkle, writing as a divorced single mother during crisis, targets the entire social system. The title itself signals this evolution: "I Want a Wife" (desiring a servant) becomes "I (Don't) Want a Wife" (rejecting the servant role, demanding society serve women).

In conclusion, Trinkle updates Brady's satirical tradition by shifting criticism from individual relationships to social structures. The comparison reveals progress in feminist discourse but stagnation in reality. Satire remains effective for exposing injustice because it makes the familiar strange. Trinkle's essay serves as both homage and warning: if society continues failing women, we remain perpetually waiting for "a functioning society."
